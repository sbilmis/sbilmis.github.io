Why this Comparison page differs
The unit of analysis is database agreement, not centre performance. Recent-paper and researcher/institution leaderboards are therefore omitted; repeating them here would invite comparisons based on unequal source coverage. OpenAIRE is shown as DOI enrichment and a separate exploratory keyword search, not as a symmetric acknowledgment corpus finder.
These three do not play the same role. Scopus and Web of Science each found this corpus independently, by searching funding-acknowledgment text — so their disagreement is evidence about coverage. OpenAIRE cannot do that: its API exposes no acknowledgment text, so it can never find a UHeM paper by acknowledgment the way the other two can. What it CAN do -- a free-text keyword search -- is attempted further down this page, honestly, false positives included. It is otherwise joined onto the Scopus corpus by DOI and describes those same papers, which is where it is genuinely stronger (open access, funder links). A three-way Venn would imply OpenAIRE's keyword search is as reliable as the other two; it is not, so this page does not draw one.

Can OpenAIRE find any of these on its own?

OpenAIRE has no acknowledgment-text field, so it cannot run the query Scopus/WoS run. What it can do is a free-text keyword search (title/description/subjects), restricted to Turkey to cut obvious noise. Why is each paper in only one database?

Every exclusive paper looked up in the other available source by DOI rather than by acknowledgment, so "missing" can be split into source-record absence and an acknowledgment-metadata gap. The table above carries the reason per paper.

“Absent from the other database” is decided by DOI lookup, and a Web of Science record may file its DOI under either of two identifier types. Reading only the first hid 55,009 DOIs — 4.7% of the snapshot, concentrated before 2008 — and inflated apparent coverage gaps at the expense of the metadata and wording explanations. Both types are read here.

Web of Science began indexing funding-acknowledgment text in August 2008. Earlier papers carry none at all, so no acknowledgment search can reach them whatever the wording — in this snapshot 0.8% of 2007 records hold any funding text, against 9.9% in 2008 and 28–38% from 2009 onward. Pre-2008 work appears here only where the record was loaded years after publication. Sparse early years are a property of the index, not evidence that little was acknowledged. A Scopus-only verdict on a pre-2008 paper therefore says nothing about whether WoS holds the record.

Read the two absence categories differently. 40 Scopus-only papers (32%) have no DOI record in the available WoS project snapshot; this is not proof that the current public WoS Core Collection lacks them. Conversely, 7 WoS-only papers (4%) were absent from Scopus when its live API diagnosis was run. The remaining 84 and 159 papers are present in the available comparison source but arrive without a usable acknowledgment.

A confirmed example is DOI 10.1142/S0218271806008735: it is absent from this project's archived WoS DOI index but visible in the current WoS Core Collection interface. Its WoS acknowledgment-field status remains unknown until the full record or an export containing funding text is inspected.

The two databases fail differently. WoS carries acknowledgment text for only 266,094 of 941,683 records with a DOI (28%) — when it has the text it keeps it in full, but often it has none. Scopus more often records something, but its fund-sponsor is a single value: a paper crediting several funders keeps one and drops the rest, which is how a TRUBA acknowledgment disappears from a paper Scopus otherwise indexes.

So neither database is simply better here, and a single-database acknowledgment study understates its corpus either way.
